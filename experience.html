Autonomous Systems Research Intern Jan 2025 to Aug 2025

University of Dayton Research Institute, Dayton, OH

The question was whether DNA can hold and move information well enough to be a candidate for next-generation and quantum computing architectures, and how that compares with silicon. I modelled it in Python, solving the Schrödinger equation to quantify information storage and transfer against silicon systems.

  • Researched DNA structure as a candidate for next-generation and quantum computing architectures, solving the Schrödinger equation in Python (NumPy and SciPy, QuTiP, Qiskit) to quantify information storage and transfer versus silicon systems.
  • Cut simulation runtime by about 90%, from roughly 48 hours a run to five, across 100+ runs. Four changes did it: vectorizing the state updates so NumPy worked in batches, swapping in an integrator that stays stable at larger steps, moving the heavy linear algebra onto the GPU, and parallelizing across runs. A 100-run study was not viable at 48 hours each, which is what made the optimization the work rather than a detour.
  • Built PyTorch surrogate models to approximate and optimize the quantum simulation dynamics, which removed the need to run the full solver for a parameter sweep at all.
  • Applied MATLAB and Simulink to the modelling, simulation and testing of autonomous vehicles and control systems.

Since then I have rebuilt and extended the work as three open-source repositories, each a tested codebase behind a manuscript: neural surrogates for DNA hole transport, a device-level comparison against resistive silicon memory, and a constraint-aware storage codec. The numbers are on the project entry, and the mistake that cost me the most is in a field log.
